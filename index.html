Readout in brief

Three directions, one recommendation

We looked at the rules core as it stands and asked one question of every setting: what does this theme change about how the game is played? We kept three and wrote a full dossier for each.

Our recommendation

Lead with Cold Salvage, on one condition: it must pass the swap test. In parallel, build the same first scenario as The Salt Road to prove the rules core is theme agnostic. If Cold Salvage fails the swap test, the Salt Road becomes the lead.

Theme one. Space

Cold Salvage

A party of four, drawn from six species, strips the wrecks of a dead ring habitat around a dying red star. Salvage keeps the tow ship flying, and the cold always collects.

Theme two. A caravan across a dead sea

The Salt Road

Four guides lead a caravan across the Hollow Sea, a salt basin where a vanished empire left glass towers, walking machines and a heat that never quite stops moving.

Theme three. A drowned coast

Tidewatch

The lamps along the Hollow Coast are going out. Four keepers walk the tideline to relight them before the fog arrives with what the sea took.

Concept art, AI generated to set direction. None of it is final, and nothing here has been playtested.

What we explored

How we got from many settings to three

  1. Read the core and the design notesWe started from what the rules core already does and the design check in the notes: any system must work under two very different skins.
  2. Sketched many settingsWe wrote a one paragraph premise for each setting we could think of and tried to say what it changes in play.
  3. Judged each against six checksThe swap test, core fit, campaign hook, art cost, distance from Gloomhaven and readability on a hex board.
  4. Built three full dossiersConcept art, rules mapping, campaign, scenarios, interface, heroes, enemies and cards for the three that survived.

What we threw away and why

Five settings we set aside

  • Fallen gods and dark fantasyThe register Gloomhaven already owns. Its store page calls it a medieval dark fantasy universe. We would be compared on a depth of content we cannot match.Set aside
  • CyberpunkThe look depends on signage, rain and crowds, which read poorly on a hex board. Our judgement is that it fights the readability check.Set aside
  • WastelandVisually close to Cold Salvage, without the pressure and air mechanics that give space something to do. It collapsed into the same board with less to say.Set aside
  • SteampunkBrass and gears are a subset of what the Salt Road’s glass machines already offer, and add no new mechanic.Set aside
  • PiratesA strong fantasy, but ships are the unit of play. Haven’s unit is one person on one hex.Set aside

Theme one. Space

Cold Salvage

Six species. One dying ring. Every hull is a clock.

A party of four, drawn from six species, strips the wrecks of a dead ring habitat around a dying red star. Salvage keeps the tow ship flying, and the cold always collects.

Tone. Quiet, industrial and tense, with dry humour between the crew.

  1. 1
    The room is the enemyAir, heat and pressure are board state. A compartment can kill you before anything shows up.
  2. 2
    Every body is a different problemEach species needs its suit for a different reason, and that reason becomes its class.
  3. 3
    The ship is homeThe tow ship Second Wind is the hub. Every repaired module is progress you can see.

Why this game

Not fantasy with lasers

Our test: swap every space noun for a fantasy one. If the scenario still plays the same, we cut it.

Fails

Kill six raiders with blasters. That is bandits with crossbows.

Passes

Cut three bulkheads before the air runs out. Every hatch you open vents the room and drags everyone one hex toward it.

Why it wins

  • The room is the enemy, so the rules have work to do beyond damage.
  • Six alien body plans that no fantasy setting can do.
  • Hard surface art is modular: the cheapest of the three themes to keep consistent, by our estimate.

What it costs

  • The busiest genre of the three, with Phantom Epoch, Rogue Angels and Stars of Akarios already there.
  • Players expect guns, so guns stay short ranged and expensive.
  • Grey corridors get dull, so every sector gets its own look and hazard.

The playable crew

Four bodies, four decks

Be the wall. The Durr wins by standing in the one hex that matters.

Human

Cutter

Run the torch hot. Small cuts store heat, the big cut spends it, if the suit holds.

Signature rule

Torch gauge

A gauge from 0 to 4 on the character mat. Torch +1 cards fill it, Spend Torch cards pay it out for bonus damage, and Vent empties it into the room as Heat. End your turn at 4 and the torch overheats: suffer 2 damage and reset to 0.

Species: none. Humans are the baseline, so the Cutter teaches the game. A hot torch also makes the Cutter the warmest suit, and Rime grows toward the warmest suit.

Experience: spend Torch 3 or more in one attack, vent 3 or more, or heal or shield an ally.

The Murmur

Tender

The healer is the medicine. The Murmur pays for every heal and helper with its own cells, then calls them home.

Signature rule

Cells

Health is a count of cells. Release spends cells to fuel a heal or to send out a scout cell. Recall brings a summon home and you heal for the health it has left. Release can never take you below 1.

Species: a hive in a frame. Damage empties the frame, so the model shows how hurt the Murmur is.

Campaign map, option A

Scenario picker

Like Gloomhaven
How it plays

Finishing a scenario unlocks new numbered locations on the ring. Pick any open one and play it. Travel is flavour.

What it does well

Simple, and the story stays in our hands. In Gloomhaven, unlocked scenarios get a sticker on the map, and travel normally draws one road event (UltraBoardGames, Dized).

The trade-off

The map is a pretty menu. Where a location sits changes nothing, which is why this one felt meaningless.

Campaign map, option B

Route through the wreck

Our recommendation
How it plays

Jump node to node, paying supply or air. Your sector sets hazards and loot. Run dry and you limp home, losing the haul.

What it does well

Every jump is a decision. Routes close as the ring decays, and some only open for a species, like the Durr hull jack.

The trade-off

More to build and balance: an economy, route events and scenarios that must work in any order. Closer to the jump map in FTL than to Gloomhaven.

Your call

Option A or option B

We would build option B. The art already promises a dying ring, and only B makes that promise cost you something.

Space gives us the cheapest consistent art, the most obvious hub and an environment that can carry the rules. The Salt Road is the most distinctive theme and the safest check that the core is theme agnostic.

  • Why not the Salt Road as the lead Runner upOnly because of cost and risk, not quality. Cloth and ornament are slower to produce well, and cultural sourcing needs review we have not arranged.
  • Why not Tidewatch as the lead Expansion candidateIt has the best atmosphere and the largest change to the rules core, plus the highest art cost. We would revisit it for an expansion.
  1. Run the swap test on five space scenario premisesOn paper, before any art or code. Any premise that survives a fantasy swap is cut.
  2. Build one scenario in Cold SalvageA single compartment with air as the clock, using only the cards and rules the core has today.
  3. Skin the same scenario data as the Salt RoadSame rules and data, new names and terrain. This tests the design check from the notes.
  4. Compare and decideIf the space version only works because of laser guns, or cannot say anything that fantasy cannot, we switch the lead.

Questions for you

Five questions that push back on the lean toward space

  1. If you could only keep one of the three, and space were off the table, which would you pick? If the answer is not space, why do you lean toward it?
  2. Space asks for hazard hexes, forced movement and an air clock. Are you willing to accept theme led rules in the core, or must every theme be a pure skin? If it is a pure skin, we should be honest that Cold Salvage is fantasy with lasers.
  3. Do you want guns to be strong? Players of space games expect them. Making them weak keeps the hex tactics interesting and may disappoint people who came for blasters.
  4. Do you want dread, awe or warmth in the first ten minutes of play? Each theme gives a different answer, and the tone should drive the choice more than the mechanics.
  5. How much risk are you willing to carry on art and review? Tidewatch costs the most to render and the Salt Road needs outside cultural review. Space is the cheapest. Are you choosing space because you want it, or because it is cheap?

A new direction for the theme

The Unasked

The Accord is a civilization of many species and very old ship Minds. It has solved hunger, money and dying by accident, and it is a little smug about it. It has not solved the edge. The Unasked are the volunteers who go there: misfits, retirees and restless young people who want to matter in a society where nobody has to.

Each job is a world that asked for help, stopped answering, or never wanted the Accord in the first place. Volunteers go in on the world's own terms, with local tools and their own wits, because that is the Accord's oldest rule, and because nobody fully trusts the Accord's help. The ship's Mind waits in orbit, runs the campaign and remembers everyone.

Tone. Clever, generous and meddling, with real danger and dry humour. Less Star Wars, more Iain M. Banks.

  1. 1
    Good, not niceThe Accord means well and meddles constantly. At the edge, meaning well is slow, expensive and sometimes fatal.
  2. 2
    Every world has its own rulesEach scenario is a new place, with its own people, ecology and trouble. Nothing repeats.
  3. 3
    A home that talks backThe ship's Mind is the campaign map, the conscience and the oldest thing the crew will ever argue with.

Why it works as a tactics game

The frontier keeps producing things that will not talk.

Why a peaceful civilization fights: the frontier keeps producing things that will not talk. Old defences still running, wildlife rewritten by failed terraforming, frightened people with weapons, and now and then a Mind that has gone its own way. Some jobs are fights. Others are a rescue, a hold or a race, with enemies as the pressure.

Why the name

The Unasked is the working title.

They go where nobody asked them, or where somebody asked far too late. The name works as a compliment and as a complaint, and the Accord hears both.

Four ways it could look

All four are grounded: real light, real materials, nothing dreamlike.

Every image is one scene made in one go. Nothing is pasted in.

Style A

Production Still

A frame from a grounded big-budget film: large-format camera, natural light, real materials, a little film grain.

The aliens read as real animals and real people, so the strange parts land harder. It is the most believable of the four.

  • slate
  • dust
  • bone
  • lamp amber
  • sea grey
References
Denis Villeneuve's Arrival and Blade Runner 2049, shot by Bradford Young and Roger Deakins, and Ridley Scott's Prometheus.
On the board
Photographic tiles look rich but can turn to noise at tile size. It needs strong value grouping and clean figure bases.

Style B

Practical Miniature

Real physical models, photographed: hand-painted resin, etched brass, practical lights and shallow focus.

It looks like the game on your table, only perfect. Every place feels built, touchable and worth looking after.

  • black stage
  • primer grey
  • brass
  • moss
  • bulb warm
References
Weta Workshop's miniatures, Douglas Trumbull's model work, and New Deal Studios' miniatures for Interstellar.
On the board
The most natural fit for a tabletop game: the art direction and the miniatures can share one look.

Style C

Restrained Painterly

Film production concept painting: visible brushwork, muted earth colours, believable light, nothing dreamlike.

Painting gives room for scale and age without the cost of photorealism. It is the easiest of the four to keep consistent.

  • umber grey
  • moss
  • ochre
  • stone
  • warm accent
References
Craig Mullins, Jama Jurabaev and Raphael Lacoste.
On the board
Brushwork survives at small sizes if values stay grouped. Effects can be painted light rather than graphic shapes.

Style D

Hard Light

Near-black, bone white and one signal orange. Hard light, huge negative space, realistic rendering, no outlines.

It turns the Minds into something monumental and slightly frightening. It is the boldest and the most recognisable.

  • near black
  • graphite
  • bone
  • signal orange
  • steel
References
Ash Thorp's design work, Joseph Kosinski's Oblivion, and Swiss International Style posters.
On the board
The cleanest board of the four: dark ground, white figures and orange for anything that matters.

My pick

My pick is Practical Miniature for the game itself, because the box and the art can share one look, with Hard Light for the Minds and the cover. Production Still is the most believable, but it is the hardest to keep consistent across hundreds of pieces.

The ship and its Mind

Nothing Is Wasted, Least Of All Time

The crew call it Least. New volunteers call it Wasted, once.

Least is ancient, patient and dry, and it will not move an inch on what matters. It treats volunteers the way a very old gardener treats seedlings: with care, with judgement, and without pretending they are trees yet. It keeps score. It will let you fail if failing teaches you something. It will not let you die cheaply.

It remembers every volunteer it has ever carried. Sometimes it answers a question you have not asked yet. Once it comforted a grieving volunteer in her dead mother's handwriting, on a steamed mirror, then apologised for a week.

Size
Forty kilometres from bow to stern.
Age
About nine thousand years.
Aboard
Forty thousand people of thirty species, in gardens, towns and lakes along its spine.
Temperament
The stable kind of Mind. It has never mutinied and never lied to a crew. It has also never once said everything it knows.

How Least appears

It shows itself in three ways.

Most of the time it is only a voice, level and unhurried, always from just behind your left shoulder.

Other Minds, for contrast

  • The rebelRespectfully, NoAn old defence Mind that walked out of the Accord's councils three hundred years ago to guard a world nobody voted to protect. It appears as a person who never blinks. Sometimes an ally, sometimes the problem.

The Mind's lieutenant

Tobiah Sen Ostrander-Vey. Everyone calls it Vey.

The size of a loaf of bread, shaped like a smooth river stone of grey ceramic. No face. A thin ring of light around its middle tilts and changes colour with its mood: amber when amused, blue when thinking, white when serious. Most crews never see the red.

Vey thinks thousands of times faster than anyone it travels with, and finds waiting almost physically painful. It has read everything. It is right about most things and insufferable about the rest.

Vey is Least's lieutenant on the ground: the Mind's hand, eyes and occasionally its tongue. Least has told it to keep the volunteers alive and never to do their job for them. Its whole story is learning to hold back. The Mind is the ocean; Vey is the wave that reaches the beach.

It never fights for you. It watches, comments, translates, and now and then points.

Twelve species to choose from

A wide roster now, so we can pick a few later.

Most are properly alien. Two are cute on purpose. One is human.

Sill

Glass giants from a world with almost no gravity.

In the party. The one who sees the crack in everything, including people.

On the board. Long reach and very fragile. Never wants anything next to it.

Biology and culture
Body
Two and a half metres tall, with hollow glass-like bones, translucent skin and a narrow blade of a head with a row of pinpoint eyes and no mouth. In normal gravity they wear brass frames or their legs would crack.
Senses and speech
They see polarised light and can read stress in a beam or a face. They speak by changing the colour of their blood: you watch a Sill talk.
Life and death
They live six hundred years. A child grows visibly inside a parent's chest, like a lantern. When a Sill dies its blood stops colouring and it goes clear.
Culture
Hurrying someone is the worst rudeness. Decisions take a season.

Hask

Rock eaters who talk with their feet.

In the party. The immovable friend. A Hask joke takes ten seconds of stamping and is always worth it.

On the board. Digs, reshapes the ground and cannot be pushed.

Biology and culture
Body
Chest high, six-limbed, plated like slate. They eat stone, and crystals grow from their plates out of whatever they ate last. No eyes.
Senses and speech
They hear through their feet and speak in stamped rhythms. Vey translates, and always editorialises.
Life and death
Children bud from the flank like pebbles. At the end of a long life a Hask sits down and becomes a boulder, and the family visits it.
Culture
Anything worth having took a long time. Builders, menders and terrible at small talk.

Kethri

Crested, hungry and incapable of lying with their crest down.

In the party. Always first through the door.

On the board. Fast. Jumps in, strikes and gets out.

Biology and culture
Body

Words come from the throat, but the meaning comes from the crest. A flattened crest turns any sentence into a question.

Culture

Vell

One person, three thousand bodies.

In the party. Unsettling and generous. Treats everyone's body as a curious cage.

On the board. Spreads over several hexes, flows through gaps, and can split in two for a while.

Biology and culture
Body

Senses and speech

Identity is negotiable. They find single bodies lonely and a little sad, and say so.

Tumble

A family of five that counts as one volunteer.

Seven places on the edge

Every job is a new world with its own rules.

Place 1

The Orchard That Asked

The picture
A world of trees the size of cathedrals, trained along trellises older than anyone can date, around a dome nobody planted.
The job
The last message said, please come. Then thirty years of silence and very good harvests. The trees are still growing.

Place 2

The Glass Steppe

The picture
A plain fused to glass by a war nobody remembers, crossed by nomads on six-legged walkers.
The job
Their herds are dying. They refuse help politely, then less politely. They would rather die than owe the Accord.

Place 3

The Buried Spires

The picture
Black engineered spires dug out of a highland, reaching further down than anyone has measured.
The job
The diggers asked the Accord to help them reach the bottom. Then they turned on the engineers who came. Nobody knows why.

Place 4

The Quiet Embassy

The picture
Doors as tall as cathedrals in the face of a glacier, with one line of warm light between them.
The job
A vanished species left it. Open the doors and it welcomes you as if you were them. Nobody who stays the night comes out quite the same.

Place 5

The Tidal Library

The picture
An archive of stacked stone terraces on a tidal flat, draining as the sea draws back at dusk.
The job
Its keeper Mind is going senile and has started rewriting the books. You have six hours between tides.

Place 6

The Lantern Reef

The picture
A living orbital reef around a gas giant, built by the Ondra, bleaching sphere by sphere.
The job
The Ondra are too slow to panic. You are not.

Place 7

Kindly Fallen

The picture
A wrecked ship in a forest valley, grown over, with a village built from its salvage.
The job
A colony that left the Accord to prove it needed nobody. It crashed and stayed. Thirty years later its children have written to ask for help. Their parents do not know.
